The Brief

Turing needed writers who could do something most writers can't: describe a visual so precisely that a generative AI model would produce it accurately — first time, every time. The work wasn't about creative writing in the traditional sense. It was about translating imagination into machine-readable instruction.

My Approach

I developed a personal framework for prompt architecture — breaking every image into layers: environment, light source, subject position, mood, camera angle, colour temperature, and negative space. Rather than writing poetically, I learned to write structurally, treating each prompt like a visual brief for a director of photography. When a model returned an output that missed the mark, I would reverse-engineer the gap: was it the lighting descriptor? The subject framing? The absence of a negative prompt? I documented each mismatch and adjusted accordingly, building a working vocabulary of what the model responded to vs. what it ignored.

What I Learned

Precision is a creative act. The most effective prompts weren't the most detailed — they were the most intentional. Knowing which details to include and which to omit is the skill. This work reshaped how I think about all writing: clarity isn't a constraint, it's the whole job.

→
Output accuracy improved consistently across prompt iterations — with refined descriptors cutting revision cycles by roughly 40% on complex scene compositions. The work is ongoing, with each new brief expanding my fluency in AI-assisted creative workflows.

The Brief

Shershaah is a biographical war film based on the life of Captain Vikram Batra — a decorated soldier who gave his life during the Kargil War. The challenge for the writing team was not dramatisation. The facts were already dramatic. The challenge was emotional truth: how do you write a soldier's tenderness, his humour, his love — in a way that makes his sacrifice hit harder, not softer?

My Contribution

Working within the broader script team, my contribution centred on character consistency — ensuring Vikram's voice felt singular across scenes, and that quieter personal moments didn't flatten into generic war-film sentiment. I worked on dialogue refinements and scene-level story beats, paying particular attention to the emotional rhythm between the conflict sequences and the personal life woven through them. As someone with a military background myself, I brought a grounded instinct for how soldiers actually speak — and what they don't say.

What I Learned

Restraint is the most powerful tool in biographical writing. The scenes that move an audience most are rarely the ones with the most words. Knowing when to stop writing — and trust the actor, the frame, the silence — is a skill I carry into every format I work in now.

→
Shershaah released to widespread critical acclaim and became one of the most-watched Hindi films on Amazon Prime Video in its opening weeks — a story that deserved to be told well, and was.

The Problem

IMS People Possible's UK enterprise clients were running email campaigns that generated opens but not action. The copy was professional — but professionally forgettable. Subject lines were descriptive rather than compelling. Body copy told recipients what the product did, not why it mattered to them, today. Click-through rates had plateaued at 33% and there was no clear strategic reason for the ceiling.

The Reframe

I approached each campaign as an editorial problem, not a marketing one. The first question I asked was: what is the reader thinking when this email lands? What's already on their mind? From that starting point, I rebuilt the email architecture — leading with a problem the reader already had, not a product the client was selling. Subject lines were rewritten as curiosity gaps or specific promises. CTAs were redesigned to feel like logical next steps, not calls to action. I ran A/B variants on tone, length, and hook style — and used the data not as a verdict but as a new brief for the next iteration.

The Insight

B2B copy fails when it speaks to an organisation instead of a person. The decision-maker reading your email at 11am on a Tuesday has problems, pressures, and priorities. The copy that cut through was always the copy that acknowledged that reality before it asked for anything.

→
CTR rose from 33% to 64% across the campaign period — nearly doubling performance on the same audience, the same list, and the same send frequency. The only variable was the copy.

The Challenge

Federal RFP writing is one of the most demanding forms of professional writing there is. The audience is a procurement panel evaluating dozens of competing proposals against a rigid scoring matrix. There's no room for personality that overshadows compliance, and no room for compliance copy so dry it loses the evaluator. Hanker Systems needed proposals that could satisfy both demands simultaneously — and win against competitors with longer track records and larger teams.

My Approach

I studied the evaluation criteria for each RFP before writing a single word — mapping every required section to the scoring rubric and identifying where differentiation was actually possible. Rather than writing to the minimum requirement, I wrote to the evaluator's question behind the requirement: not just "what is your delivery methodology" but "why should we trust this team to execute under pressure?" For SaaS clients — Oracle, Salesforce, SAP — I translated technical capability into outcomes language, ensuring the proposal spoke the language of value, not just features. Each pitch deck was structured as a narrative: a problem the agency had, a solution we uniquely offered, and evidence we'd done it before.

What I Discovered

Federal procurement panels aren't moved by enthusiasm. They're moved by precision, specificity, and evidence. The proposals that won were the ones that made the evaluator's job easier — clear, scannable, and structured so that every claim was backed before it was made.

→
Set a company record for successful proposals in the first four months. Win rate improved by 20%. Over $70,000 in US federal contracts secured — directly attributable to the quality of the written submission.

The Context

OTT platforms live and die on the scroll. A viewer on Hotstar or Voot has 400 titles competing for the same moment of attention. The thumbnail gets them to stop. The synopsis gets them to click. The promo gets them to stay. At Prime Focus Technologies, I was responsible for all three — writing the metadata, promotional copy, and on-platform descriptions for over 500 titles across three years, spanning flagship reality formats, prime-time drama, and acquired international content.

The Work

Working embedded within production and creative teams gave me something most copywriters don't get: access to the content before it was edited. I sat with post-production on Bigg Boss cuts, understood the emotional arc of each episode, and wrote promos that teased the right moment — the one that would make a non-viewer curious and a regular viewer certain they couldn't miss it. For Naagin, I developed a consistent tone language that balanced supernatural spectacle with emotional stakes. For Roadies, it was urgency and tribalism — the copy had to feel like it was written for someone already invested. Alongside copy, I led thumbnail selection — choosing frames that read as a story in a single image, not just a good still.

What Scaled

Volume teaches you to trust instinct. After 500 titles, I could read a piece of content and identify the hook in the first pass — because I had made the same decision, correctly and incorrectly, hundreds of times before. That pattern recognition is something no brief can give you.
